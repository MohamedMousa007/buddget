1 · The campaign concept
What's actually left?
فاضل فعلًا كام؟

One story told in three acts. The campaign asks one question and then answers it. The wording never changes, only how much of Buddget is shown.

The insight owner belief

People check the bank balance, but the balance is the number before the instalments, subscriptions, rent and goals. What they really want to know is what is actually left. This is a belief to validate in interviews, not a statistic.

Why this concept wins

  • It's a question people already ask, so it feels recognised, not invented.
  • It carries no shame: everyone asks it.
  • It works in all three dialects and all year.
  • It gives Buddgy a natural job: asking.
Challenge. "What's left" is close to the safe-to-spend wording Masrofi, Masareef and Masareefy already use, so the concept can't carry a feature claim. It carries a point of view. Act 2 widens "what's left" into "the whole picture", and that's where we differ. The picture has to be real before Act 3 says so.

Mapping to the earlier phases: Act 1 covers problem awareness and curiosity. Act 2 covers brand awareness. Act 3 covers reveal, pre-launch, launch and post-launch.

  • Primary: teal. Secondary: ink and mist, with teal soft for the marker. Red only for warnings and negatives.
  • Balance: mostly mist and ink. A teal ground in no more than about one frame in three, so teal stays the signature and doesn't turn into wallpaper.
  • Type: DM Sans for English, IBM Plex Sans Arabic for Arabic, JetBrains Mono for numbers, all already in the app. No other fonts.
  • Layout: one idea per frame; the question or headline in the upper two-thirds; sign-off at the bottom; Arabic right to left with the wordmark and numbers left to right; text inside platform safe zones.
  • Motion: calm, about 1.5 to 2.5 seconds a beat; leaders draw on, numbers tick, the marker wipes; Buddgy changes mood and doesn't dance; one soft tick for sound. A motion designer sets exact timings.
  • Logo and Buddgy: exactly as designed. No outline, shadow, recolour or new pose.
  • Not added: no new characters, icons, illustrations, stock people, gradients, emoji, 3D, extra colours or extra fonts.

4 · Buddgy and the helmet joke

Decision: remove the helmet joke

The joke. «Even I'd lose track, and I'm a helmet.»

  • Too cute or childish? Slightly yes. A character mentioning its own body is the standard mascot move, and it nudges Buddgy toward a toy.
  • Does it strengthen or weaken the character? It weakens it. It makes Buddgy the subject. The best humour here is about the viewer's situation.
  • Does it fit the personality? Partly: the dryness fits. The target doesn't. And "helmet" in Arabic (خوذة) carries military or construction associations, not a friendly one.
  • Verdict: remove, and keep the dry register. Replace it with a joke about the situation.

Replacement: «Five places, one salary. Even I'd need a minute.» Arabic (Egypt, draft): خمس أماكن لمرتب واحد. حتى أنا هحتاج دقيقة.

Keep · humour about the situation

Personality

  • Curious, not clever. Buddgy asks.
  • Calm. Never rushed, never alarmed.
  • Plainspoken. Short sentences, no jargon.
  • Dryly warm. Understated, includes the viewer.
  • Never judging. No opinion about anyone's spending.

Role

Buddgy is Buddget's way of asking. Buddgy asks questions, notices the situation and reacts. Buddget, the wordmark voice or a real screen, states the facts, claims and numbers. This split protects credibility. The test for any post: if Buddgy were removed, would it still read as a credible finance brand? If not, rewrite it.

When Buddgy appears

  • Act 1: in about one post in three, small, as the closing beat.
  • Act 2: up to one post in two while the character is introduced, then settle back.
  • Act 3: as a pointer in demos. Never covering the screen.
  • Never in every frame, never the whole post.

How Buddgy communicates

  • Short type bubbles, 12 words or fewer, mostly questions.
  • No cartoon voice, no sound effects, no emoji.
  • Moods: curious (asks), thinking (counting), done (clear), wink (dry humour), listening (Q&A), talking (explains), sleepy (night sign-off).
  • Never alert, worried or error faces in awareness content.
  • Existing artwork and mood animations only. No new poses.

Humour that's allowed

  • Dry observation about a situation ("five places, one salary").
  • Self-inclusive understatement ("even I'd need a minute").
  • Playful use of numbers.

Humour that's not

  • Anything about Buddgy's body or appearance.
  • Jokes about being poor, broke or bad with money.
  • Sarcasm toward the viewer.
  • Trend memes and slang that will age.
  • Religion, family pressure or giving.

6 · Native Arabic reviewers: sourcing and budget

Hire a native copywriter, not a translator

Which role

A native social-media copywriter who localises ("transcreates") and reviews, not a translator and not just a proofreader. A translator converts words. We need someone who fixes dialect, humour and tone, and rewrites a line when it sounds translated.

Qualifications to look for

  • Native speaker of that dialect, raised or living in the market.
  • Social copy portfolio (Reels, TikTok scripts, captions), not only articles.
  • Comfortable with humour and informal tone.
  • Finance or consumer-app familiarity is a plus, not a must.
  • Replies within about 48 hours.
  • Can explain why they changed a line.

Where to find them

What they review, and what they don't

Review

  • On-screen text and carousel copy
  • Captions and calls to action
  • Reel and TikTok scripts and voice-over lines
  • Story polls and questions
  • The final designed assets, to catch right-to-left text, line-break and punctuation errors
  • Humour, tone and sensitive topics

Not their job

  • Product facts and claims. The owner checks these against the brief.
  • Legal or financial advice wording.
  • Visual design.

Revisions and final approval

1 · Draft
AI-assisted draft in the market's dialect, and you check it against the claims list.
2 · Review
The reviewer edits in a shared document and marks each line: OK, edited, replaced or flagged.
3 · Revise
One round of revisions is included in the batch fee. Language and dialect calls belong to the reviewer. Brand and claims calls belong to you.
4 · Proof
The reviewer checks the finished designs for text-rendering problems.
5 · Approve
You give final approval. The reviewer's name and date go beside the post in the calendar. Nothing publishes without both.

How to start

  1. Egypt: shortlist 3 candidates on Upwork and send each a short paid test, about 3 of the 5 posts from the first batch, including one joke and one financial term. Pay each a small fixed fee, then pick one. Treat it as a trial and keep a second as backup.
  2. Saudi and UAE: start sourcing when the first posts for those markets are scheduled. No Saudi or UAE post is published before its own native review.
  3. Keep a shared glossary per dialect: balance, instalment, subscription, salary, "what's left". It grows with every batch.
  4. Reviewers see only marketing drafts. They are not given product data or user information.
